How this implantation calculator works

Implantation timing is counted from ovulation, not from your last period, because it is the age of the fertilised egg that matters. Here is the exact arithmetic.

Ovulation day = your ovulation date, or LMP + (cycle length − 14)
Implantation window = ovulation day + 6 days → ovulation day + 12 days
Most likely implantation = ovulation day + 9 days
Earliest reliable test = most likely implantation + 3 days
Week 0 (LMP) = ovulation day − 14 days, unless a real LMP was entered
  • Default assumption. The calculator assumes a 14-day luteal phase, so if you only know your last period and cycle length, ovulation is placed at LMP + (cycle length − 14). For a 28-day cycle that is day 14.
  • Why 6 to 12 days? A fertilised egg normally reaches the uterus and begins to implant somewhere between six and twelve days after ovulation. The window is a range because the exact day varies between pregnancies.
  • Why day 9? Nine days after ovulation is the most commonly reported single day, so it is used as the centre of the estimate — not as a guarantee.
  • Why three extra days for testing? After implantation the body starts producing hCG, and it takes a few days for the amount in urine to reach the level a home test can detect. Three days is a cautious allowance.
  • All date maths uses whole UTC calendar days, so daylight-saving changes can never shift a result by a day.

Dates that do not exist, such as 30 February, and dates in the future are rejected instead of being quietly adjusted.

Reading the day-by-day table

Each row is one day after your estimated ovulation date. Days 6 to 12 are the period in which implantation may occur; the highlighted rows mark the centre of the window and the earliest sensible test date.

Light spotting or mild cramping is sometimes reported around implantation, and is sometimes called implantation bleeding. It is usually lighter and shorter than a period, but many people have no symptoms at all. Neither the presence nor the absence of symptoms can confirm implantation, and this page cannot detect it.

If a test is negative but your period has not arrived, wait two or three days and test again. Home tests measure hCG in urine, and the level roughly doubles every two to three days in early pregnancy, so a second test a few days later is much more informative than a very early one.

When to talk to a clinician

Contact a doctor, midwife or early pregnancy unit if any of the following applies:

  • You have a positive test with pain, cramping on one side, or bleeding.
  • You feel dizzy, faint, or have shoulder-tip pain — seek urgent medical help.
  • Your period is more than a week late and repeated tests stay negative.
  • You have heavy bleeding that soaks a pad an hour, or bleeding with fever.
  • You have a known fertility concern, or you have had previous ectopic pregnancies or miscarriages.
Not medical advice. Early Pregnancy Tools is an educational calculator. It cannot detect implantation, diagnose a pregnancy, or rule one out. Always rely on a clinician and a pregnancy test for medical decisions.

Frequently asked questions

When does implantation happen?

Implantation usually happens 6 to 12 days after ovulation, most often around 9 days. This calculator centres the estimate on day 9 and shows the full window so you can see the range.

How is the earliest test date chosen?

The suggested date is three days after the most likely implantation day, which is when the hormone hCG is usually high enough for a home urine test to detect. Testing earlier than that can produce a false negative.

Can I feel implantation?

Some people notice light spotting or mild cramping around this time, but many notice nothing at all. Symptoms alone cannot confirm implantation, and this calculator cannot detect it.

Does a later implantation date mean something is wrong?

Not by itself. The window is a range and most pregnancies implant within it. If you have pain, bleeding, or a test that stays negative long after your period was due, speak to a clinician.
